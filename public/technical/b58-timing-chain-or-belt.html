B58 Timing Chain or Belt? The Definitive Answer

The BMW B58 3.0-litre petrol engine uses a timing chain, not a belt. This page explains the chain design, location, service life, common issues, and what to look for to avoid expensive repairs.

The BMW B58 engine uses a timing chain, not a timing belt. Unlike the problematic N47 diesel, the B58 chain is front-mounted and significantly more reliable, but it can still suffer from tensioner wear or chain stretch, particularly on high-mileage or poorly maintained examples.

  • Engine: B58B30 / B58M30 (3.0-litre petrol)
  • Timing system: Chain-driven (not a belt)
  • BMW official interval: No scheduled replacement — but specialist advice suggests inspection at 80,000–100,000 miles [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • Key warning: Cold-start rattle is the most common early symptom — don't ignore it
  • Cost: Chain replacement: £1,500–£2,800 at independent specialists [EM-QUOTE]

Design & Location: B58 Chain Is Front-Mounted

The B58 engine benefits from a significantly improved timing chain design compared to earlier BMW engines like the N47 diesel.

The B58's timing chain is front-mounted, located at the front of the engine behind the timing cover. This makes it far more accessible than the rear-mounted chain of the N47, which required engine removal or a transmission-out process for replacement. The B58 uses a robust chain design with improved tensioners and guides, greatly reducing the catastrophic failure rate seen on older engines.

The chain is driven by the crankshaft via a sprocket. It uses a hydraulic tensioner that relies on engine oil pressure to maintain correct tension. On some B58 variants (e.g., X3 M40i, 5 Series 540i), there is also a secondary balancer shaft chain that should be inspected when the primary chain is being serviced.

For more detail on the B58's design and how it compares to earlier engines, see our B58 Engine Knowledge Centre and the B58 vs N55 comparison page.

Service Life: How Long Does the B58 Timing Chain Last?

BMW does not specify a service interval for the B58 timing chain. The official line is that the chain is designed to last the lifetime of the engine. However, independent specialists who work on these engines regularly report that chain wear and tensioner fatigue can occur from around 80,000 miles, particularly on cars that have had extended oil change intervals or have been used for short urban journeys.

The B58 is far more reliable than its diesel counterpart, the N47, but it is not immune to timing chain issues. The most common cause of premature wear is oil degradation — if the oil is not changed regularly, sludge can build up in the tensioner, reducing its ability to maintain tension. This is why many specialists recommend reducing the oil change interval to 8,000–10,000 miles rather than relying on the 18,000-mile CBS system. Symptoms of Timing Chain Wear

Early detection is the key to avoiding a catastrophic engine failure. The following symptoms should prompt an immediate inspection:

  • Cold-start rattle — a metallic clattering sound that lasts 1–2 seconds on startup, then disappears. This is the most common and earliest warning sign.
  • Engine warning light — camshaft position sensor codes (e.g., P0016) or timing correlation faults.
  • Rough idle or misfire — if the chain has stretched enough to affect timing, the engine may run unevenly.
  • Loss of power / hesitation — particularly noticeable under acceleration as valve timing becomes less precise.
  • Oil sludge or metallic particles in the oil filter — a sign of chain guide wear.
  • Check engine light with cam/crank correlation codes — P0016, P0017, P000A, P000B are common.

If you hear a cold-start rattle, do not ignore it. A chain that fails while driving can cause catastrophic valve-to-piston contact, resulting in a full engine replacement. For a full diagnostic guide, see our cold-start rattle symptom hub and the B58 timing chain guide.

Prevention and Early Detection

While the B58 is significantly more reliable than earlier engines, there are still steps you can take to minimise the risk of timing chain failure.

  • Shorten oil change intervals — reduce from 18,000 miles to 8,000–10,000 miles, especially if the car is used for short journeys or sporty driving.
  • Use the correct oil — BMW Longlife-01 or Longlife-04 oil is essential to prevent sludge buildup in the tensioner. See our B58 oil capacity page for full specs.
  • Listen for cold-start rattle — the single most important early warning sign. If you hear it, get the car inspected immediately.
  • Monitor the oil level and quality — low oil or dirty oil can starve the tensioner of pressure.
  • Inspect at 80,000–100,000 miles — even if there are no symptoms, a preventative inspection by a specialist can catch wear before it becomes catastrophic.
  • Avoid extended idling — prolonged idling can cause oil pressure drops that may affect the tensioner.
