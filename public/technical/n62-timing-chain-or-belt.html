N62 Timing Chain Or Belt?

The BMW 4.4‑litre V8 uses a timing chain — duplex primary, simplex secondary, with hydraulic tensioners and plastic guide rails that can wear over time. This page gives the definitive specification, the high‑mileage failure pattern, and what to check before buying.

Yes — the N62 4.4 V8 uses a timing chain, not a belt. The primary drive is a duplex roller chain between the crankshaft and the intermediate shaft, and from there to each cylinder bank via simplex chains. The system is robust but the plastic guide rails and hydraulic tensioners are known to wear, particularly on high‑mileage examples.

  • Design: Duplex primary, simplex secondary, hydraulic tensioners [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs workshop manual]
  • Typical failure: Guide‑rail wear, tensioner bleed‑down, chain stretch on neglected oil service [PATTERN DATA]
  • Inspection cost: ~£150–£250 for a specialist to check guides and tensioner [EM‑QUOTE]
  • Full chain replacement: £2,500–£4,500 at an independent specialist [EM‑QUOTE]

Notes & Caveats

The N62 timing chain system is fundamentally durable — unlike the later N63, the N62 does not suffer from catastrophic guide‑rail failure on every car. However, the plastic guides do become brittle with age and heat cycles, and once a guide breaks, the chain can jump, bending valves almost instantly. Independent specialists we've spoken to [EM‑INTERVIEW] report that most failures occur on cars that have had extended oil‑change intervals (BMW's 15,000‑mile "condition‑based" service) — the oil loses its ability to maintain tensioner pressure, allowing the chain to slap against the guides. This is one of the few engines where preventative guide inspection at 80,000 miles is genuinely cost‑effective.

For the full consequence of ignoring chain wear, see the N62 valve‑stem seal failure page — the two issues often co‑occur on neglected cars.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming it's a belt. It is not — the N62 has never used a timing belt. A belt‑driven camshaft was a feature of the M60/M62, not the N62.
  • Trusting the service computer blindly. The CBS interval of 15,000 miles is too long for this engine if you plan to keep it beyond 100,000 miles — shorten to 8,000‑10,000 and always use BMW LL‑01 approved oil.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on start‑up (< 2 seconds) is often the tensioners bleeding down; a persistent rattle is guide‑wear. If you hear it, get it inspected before it snaps.
  • Confusing N62 with N63. The later N63 uses a very similar chain drive but with a different tensioner location and a known valve‑stem oil‑consumption issue; see our N63 timing chain specification for the comparison.
  • Replacing just the chain. If the guides are worn, they have to come out — and once you're in there, a full kit (chain + guides + tensioner + cam sprocket bolts) is standard practice.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The N62 timing covers are at the front of the engine, behind the alternator and the water pump. A specialist can inspect the chain and guides via the oil‑fill cap and by using a borescope through the timing‑chain inspection hole on the left‑hand bank — but the only definitive check is to open the timing cover. The recommended method is:

  1. Cold‑start rattle test — listen for more than 2 seconds of rattle.
  2. Oil pressure check — low pressure at idle can indicate a failing tensioner.
  3. Borescope inspection — view the guides through the front timing cover plug.
  4. If in doubt, drop the oil pan — plastic debris from guides is the final warning sign.

Full details in the Engine Number Location guide — confirm you have an N62 before ordering parts.

Why This Specification Matters

The N62 was BMW's first all‑aluminium V8, and its chain drive was a major step forward — but it's also the Achilles heel on neglected cars. Unlike a belt, a chain does not have a mandatory replacement interval, so owners assume it lasts forever. The reality is that the plastic guides and hydraulic tensioners are consumables. If a guide fails at speed, the chain jumps, and the engine is effectively destroyed. The repair cost of a full chain replacement (£2,500–£4,500) is often 40–60% of the car's value on a high‑mileage 5‑Series or X5, which is why the 70% BMW scrap threshold applies — if the chain has snapped, the engine is a write‑off; if it's caught early, it's an expensive but viable repair.

This is also why the economics of 5‑Series engine replacement page exists — the decision is based on whether you're dealing with a snapped chain (scrap) or worn guides (repair).
