S55 Timing Chain Or Belt?

The BMW S55 3.0‑litre twin‑turbo straight‑six (found in the F80 M3, F82 M4, and F87 M2 Competition) uses a timing chain — duplex primary, simplex secondary, with hydraulic tensioners and plastic guide rails. This page gives the definitive specification, the high‑mileage failure pattern, and what to check before buying.

Yes — the S55 uses a timing chain, not a belt. The primary drive is a duplex roller chain between the crankshaft and the intermediate shaft, and from there to the camshafts via simplex chains. The system is robust but the plastic guide rails and hydraulic tensioners can wear on high‑mileage or track‑used cars.

  • Design: Duplex primary, simplex secondary, hydraulic tensioners [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs workshop manual]
  • Typical failure: Guide‑rail wear, tensioner bleed‑down, chain stretch on neglected oil service [PATTERN DATA]
  • Inspection cost: ~£250–£400 for a specialist to check guides and tensioner [EM‑QUOTE]
  • Full chain replacement: £3,500–£5,500 at an independent specialist [EM‑QUOTE]

Notes & Caveats

The S55 timing chain system is fundamentally durable — unlike the N47, the S55 does not suffer from catastrophic guide‑rail failure on every car. However, the plastic guides do become brittle with age and heat cycles, and once a guide breaks, the chain can jump, bending valves almost instantly. Independent specialists we've spoken to [EM‑INTERVIEW] report that most failures occur on cars that have had extended oil‑change intervals (BMW's 15,000‑mile "condition‑based" service) — the oil loses its ability to maintain tensioner pressure, allowing the chain to slap against the guides. This is one of the few engines where preventative guide inspection at 80,000 miles is genuinely cost‑effective, especially if the car has seen track use.

For the full consequence of ignoring chain wear, see the S55 crank‑hub failure page — the two issues often co‑occur on neglected cars, as both are linked to oil pressure and timing integrity.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming it's a belt. It is not — the S55 has never used a timing belt. A belt‑driven camshaft was a feature of the M60/M62, not the S55.
  • Trusting the service computer blindly. The CBS interval of 15,000 miles is too long for this engine if you plan to keep it beyond 100,000 miles — shorten to 8,000‑10,000 and always use BMW LL‑01 approved oil.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on start‑up (< 2 seconds) is often the tensioners bleeding down; a persistent rattle is guide‑wear. If you hear it, get it inspected before it snaps.
  • Confusing S55 with N55. The earlier N55 uses a very similar chain drive but with a different tensioner location and different failure modes; see our N55 timing chain specification for the comparison.
  • Replacing just the chain. If the guides are worn, they have to come out — and once you're in there, a full kit (chain + guides + tensioner + cam sprocket bolts) is standard practice.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The S55 timing covers are at the front of the engine, behind the alternator and the water pump. A specialist can inspect the chain and guides via the oil‑fill cap and by using a borescope through the timing‑chain inspection hole on the left‑hand bank — but the only definitive check is to open the timing cover. The recommended method is:

  1. Cold‑start rattle test — listen for more than 2 seconds of rattle.
  2. Oil pressure check — low pressure at idle can indicate a failing tensioner.
  3. Borescope inspection — view the guides through the front timing cover plug.
  4. If in doubt, drop the oil pan — plastic debris from guides is the final warning sign.

Why This Specification Matters

The S55 is BMW's high‑performance version of the N55, and its chain drive is a critical part of the engine's ability to sustain high revs and track abuse. Unlike a belt, a chain does not have a mandatory replacement interval, so owners assume it lasts forever. The reality is that the plastic guides and hydraulic tensioners are consumables. If a guide fails at speed, the chain jumps, and the engine is effectively destroyed. The repair cost of a full chain replacement (£3,500–£5,500) is often 40–60% of the car's value on a high‑mileage M3 or M4, which is why the 70% BMW scrap threshold applies — if the chain has snapped, the engine is a write‑off; if it's caught early, it's an expensive but viable repair.

This is also why the economics of 3‑Series engine replacement page exists — the decision is based on whether you're dealing with a snapped chain (scrap) or worn guides (repair).
