BMW M57 Timing Chain Or Belt

Does the BMW M57 3.0L diesel use a timing chain or a timing belt? The definitive answer — plus specifications, interval, tensioner design, and what every owner needs to know.

The BMW M57 3.0L diesel engine uses a duplex timing chain (not a belt) [THIRD-PARTY — BMW TIS]. The chain is located at the front of the engine and drives the camshafts, injection pump, and ancillary components. It is designed for the life of the engine under normal conditions, but tensioner wear and oil-related issues can cause stretch or failure on high-mileage units.

  • Chain type: Duplex (double-row) roller chain – robust design, less prone to stretch than simplex.
  • Replacement interval: Not officially specified – condition-based. Most specialists recommend inspection at 150,000 miles or when cold-start rattle appears [EM-INTERVIEW].
  • Key warning signs: Cold-start rattle, timing faults (P0016), metal particles in oil.
  • Unlike the later N57, the M57's chain is front-mounted and more accessible.

Timing Chain Design & Operation

The M57 timing chain is a robust duplex design that drives two overhead camshafts and the injection pump.

Unlike the later N57 diesel, which uses a simplex (single-row) chain mounted at the rear of the engine, the M57's duplex chain is mounted at the front. This makes it significantly more accessible for inspection and replacement, though the job still requires timing tools and specialist knowledge. The chain drives the camshafts via sprockets on the camshafts and crankshaft, and also drives the injection pump (depending on variant – early M57 with VP44 rotary pump, or later common-rail with a separate high-pressure pump). The chain is tensioned by a hydraulic tensioner, which uses engine oil pressure to maintain correct tension. The tensioner also has a mechanical ratchet mechanism to prevent the chain from becoming loose when the engine is off.

The duplex design provides redundancy – even if one row of the chain wears, the other can often maintain timing long enough to prevent catastrophic failure, though this is not a guarantee. Many specialists consider the M57 chain to be one of the more durable BMW timing chain designs, with failures being far less common than on the N47.

Real-World Caveats

While the M57 timing chain is robust, there are known issues that can affect its longevity. The most common cause of chain failure is tensioner wear – the hydraulic tensioner relies on good oil pressure and clean oil. If the oil is not changed regularly or the engine has low oil pressure (e.g., due to worn bearings or a failing oil pump), the tensioner may not apply sufficient force, allowing the chain to slap against the guides and eventually stretch or jump timing.

Another issue is guide wear – the plastic-faced guides can crack or wear over time, especially on high-mileage engines. This can cause the chain to run out of alignment, leading to wear on the sprockets and eventual failure. Many specialists recommend replacing the guides, tensioner, and chain as a set when any work is done on the timing system – this is covered in more detail on our timing chain diagnosis guide.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the M57 has a timing belt. Many owners confuse the M57 with older BMW petrol engines (M40/M42) which used belts – the M57 is chain-driven.
  • Ignoring cold-start rattle. A brief rattle on startup (1–2 seconds) is often the first sign of a worn tensioner. Ignoring it can lead to chain stretch and eventual failure.
  • Using the wrong oil spec. The M57 requires BMW Longlife-04 (5W-30) – using a thicker oil (10W-40) can reduce oil pressure to the tensioner, increasing wear.
  • Replacing the chain without the tensioner. The tensioner is a wear item – always replace it when doing a chain job. A new chain with an old tensioner will fail prematurely.
  • Not checking oil pressure. If a chain fails due to tensioner wear, the root cause may be low oil pressure – check the oil pump and bearings before fitting a new chain.

How to Verify Chain Health

The first step in verifying the health of the M57 timing chain is to listen for a rattle on cold start. A brief rattle (less than 1 second) is often normal, but anything longer than 2 seconds indicates a tensioner or chain issue. The next step is to inspect the chain tensioner – it's located on the front of the engine and can be removed with a 32mm socket. If the tensioner is extended more than 15mm (specification varies), the chain may have stretched.

For a definitive check, a specialist can measure the chain stretch using diagnostic software (timing correction values) or by physically checking the timing marks on the crankshaft and camshafts. If the chain has jumped time, you'll likely have fault codes such as P0016 (crankshaft/camshaft correlation).
