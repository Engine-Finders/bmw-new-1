BMW Coolant Specification

The approved coolant specification for BMW engines across 1990–2025: G48, HT-12, colour codes, capacities and the mixing rules that matter.

BMW's factory coolant specification is either G48 (blue/green) or HT-12 (green) depending on model year. Both are ethylene-glycol-based, nitrite- and amine-free, and must be mixed 50:50 with distilled water.

  • G48 — BMW part 83 19 2 282 225 — for most models up to ~2018 [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs BMW parts]
  • HT-12 — BMW part 83 19 2 450 844 — for newer models (2018+), includes silicates [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs BMW parts]
  • Capacity: typically 8–12 litres total, varies by engine — see table below
  • Change interval: BMW states 4 years / 60,000 miles on G48, up to 5 years on HT-12 [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs BMW workshop data]
  • Never mix G48 and HT-12 — gel formation and cooling system failure

Notes & Caveats

The 50:50 mix ratio is not optional. Using anything other than distilled water introduces minerals that accelerate corrosion and can block the cooling system — a £15 mistake that can cost a radiator or water pump. Independent specialists we've spoken to consistently report that many owners don't realise BMW's coolant spec changed with the B-series engines: HT-12 is not backward-compatible with G48, and mixing them causes the additives to gel, blocking the heater matrix and radiator [EM-INTERVIEW]. This is covered in full mechanism detail on our thermostat housing failure page and coolant loss without a leak symptom guide.

Common Mistakes

  • Mixing G48 and HT-12. The gelling that results is not a "it might be fine" risk — it will block the heater matrix and radiator.
  • Using tap water. Tap water introduces calcium and other minerals that scale the system and reduce cooling efficiency — the difference between a £20 coolant change and a £400 radiator.
  • Believing colour alone tells you the spec. Both G48 and HT-12 appear blue/green or green depending on batch. The colour is a dye, not a reliable identifier — always check the part number on the bottle.
  • Using a non-BMW approved "universal" coolant. Most universal coolants do not meet the silicate or borate package required by BMW engines — they protect the block but not the water pump or heater core.
  • Ignoring the interval. The additives in G48 exhaust after four years — the coolant still looks clean but has lost its corrosion protection. This is why the water pump failure and thermostat housing pages show early failures on cars with overdue coolant.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The coolant specification for your specific chassis and engine code is stamped on the expansion tank (G48) or listed on the sticker under the bonnet on later HT-12 cars. If in doubt, the BMW engine number decoder page can confirm the build date and factory fill spec — Engine Code Decoder. For a definitive answer, the BMW parts system using the last seven digits of your VIN will tell you exactly which coolant was factory-filled.

Why This Specification Matters

The coolant specification affects the water pump, thermostat housing, EGR cooler and heater matrix all at once. Using the wrong coolant doesn't cause a single, dramatic failure — it reduces the life of every component in the cooling circuit, and the bill when it all fails together is far higher than the difference between a £20 universal coolant and a £40 BMW-approved bottle. The chemistry is not marketing: the additive package is specifically designed for BMW's aluminium and plastic cooling circuit, and the absence of nitrates and amines is part of the water pump seal life calculation. This is why specialists we've spoken to treat "any green coolant" as a red flag — see our timing chain diagnosis guide for a parallel example of why BMW specification matters beyond the spec sheet.
