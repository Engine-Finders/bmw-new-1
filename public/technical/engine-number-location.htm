BMW engine numbers are stamped on the engine block. Location varies by family: M-series — front-left or right side; N-series — left side near oil filter housing; B-series — top left near the timing cover; S-series — same as base engine family.

  • Primary method: Engine block stamp — use a torch and mirror for hard-to-read positions.
  • Secondary method: VIN decode — positions 4–7 of the VIN reveal the engine code.
  • Tertiary method: Build sheet sticker — under bonnet or driver's door jamb.
  • Full engine number includes code + variant + unique serial number.

Location by Engine Family — Detailed Guide

Each BMW engine family places the engine number stamp in a consistent location. Use these descriptions to locate yours.

  • M-series (M20, M30, M40, M42, M43, M44): The stamp is usually on a machined pad on the left or right side of the block, just above the oil pan rail. For M20/M30, it's often on the passenger side (UK right), near the engine mount. For M40/M42, it's on the driver's side (UK left), accessible from below.
  • M47/M57 (diesel): Look on the front-left side of the block, below the injection pump and near the oil filter housing. The stamp is on a flat machined area and is often visible from above with a torch.
  • N47/N57 (diesel): The stamp is on the left side of the block, just behind the oil filter housing. On some models, the air intake pipe may need to be moved for clear access.
  • N20/N55 (petrol): Located on the left side of the block, below the intake manifold. Sometimes obscured by vacuum lines or the intake plenum. A small sticker on the timing cover may also display the engine code, but the stamp is definitive.
  • N63/S63 (V8): The stamp is on the top front of the engine, between the cylinder banks. This area is covered by the engine beauty covers and intake plumbing — removal is required. The stamp is on a flat pad near the front of the valley.
  • B47/B57/B58 (modular): The stamp is on the top left of the block, near the timing chain cover. The engine cover must be removed. A sticker on the timing cover also lists the engine code and variant.

For exact placement, refer to the engine code decoder to confirm your engine family first.

VIN Decoding as an Alternative Method

If the engine stamp is difficult to read, the VIN (Vehicle Identification Number) is a reliable backup. The engine code is embedded in positions 4–7 of the VIN. For example, a VIN beginning with WBA7B... often decodes to a B58 engine. However, this method only gives the engine family and base code, not the variant suffix or serial number.

For a complete decode, use our VIN decoder tool, which extracts the engine code, build date, and factory options. BMW dealerships can also provide the full engine number from the VIN using their parts system.

The engine stamp remains the definitive source — the VIN tells you what was factory-fitted, not what is in the car now if the engine has been replaced.

Build Sheet Location

Many BMWs have a build sheet sticker (often white or silver) located in one of three places:

  • Under the bonnet: On the underside of the bonnet itself, or on the strut tower (left or right).
  • Driver's door jamb: On the B-pillar or the door edge, near the tyre pressure label.
  • Inside the service booklet: A paper build sheet is often included with the service history documents.

The build sheet lists the engine code, variant, and sometimes the full serial number. This is a quick way to confirm the engine code without crawling under the car, but it's not a substitute for the physical stamp — build sheets can be lost or misprinted.

Real-World Caveats

The engine number stamp can be difficult to read due to corrosion, dirt, or paint. A wire brush and some penetrating oil can help reveal a faded stamp. On some engines, the stamp is on a curved surface, making it harder to photograph or transcribe accurately.

If the engine has been replaced, the stamp will not match the VIN — this is common on N47 timing chain failure cars. In this case, rely on the stamp on the installed engine. The stamp is the legal engine number used for DVLA records and MOT inspections.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the VIN with the engine number. The VIN is on the windscreen or chassis; the engine number is on the block. They are different.
  • Assuming the build sheet is always correct. Build sheets can be lost, damaged, or from a different car if the car was repaired.
  • Looking in the wrong place for the engine family. Always confirm the engine family first using the engine code decoder — the location varies significantly between M, N, B, and S series.
  • Not cleaning the stamp area. Dirt and grease can obscure the stamp. A clean with a degreaser and a wire brush can make it legible.
  • Trusting an online VIN decoder that only gives the code, not the full serial. Most free decoders only give the engine family; they don't provide the unique serial number stamped on the block.

How to Verify the Engine Number on the Vehicle

Step-by-step verification:

  1. Park the car on level ground and allow the engine to cool (if checking the top of the block).
  2. Remove any engine covers — for B and N series, the decorative top cover must be removed. For N63/S63, both the upper and lower covers may need to be taken off.
  3. Use a torch and, if needed, an inspection mirror to find the stamp pad. For M-series engines on the side of the block, the car may need to be raised on axle stands.
  4. Clean the area with a degreaser and wire brush to reveal the stamped numbers.
  5. Record the full code — it includes the family letter, displacement, fuel type, variant suffix, and a 5–7 digit serial number.
  6. Cross-check with the VIN using our VIN decoder to confirm the factory-specified engine family matches the stamp.
