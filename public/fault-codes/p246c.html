P246C BMW — DPF Pressure Sensor Range/Performance

The ECU has detected that the differential pressure sensor signal for the diesel particulate filter is out of its expected range or performing unreliably. This often means a failing sensor, but it can also point to wiring issues or a blocked DPF.

P246C means the DPF differential pressure sensor is reporting a voltage or pressure reading that falls outside the expected range. In most BMW diesels, this is a sensor or wiring fault — not a blocked filter. A replacement sensor typically costs £180–£380 fitted [PATTERN DATA], while a blocked DPF can cost £1,200–£2,200.

  • Most common real cause: failed differential pressure sensor — £180–£380 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Worst case: blocked DPF causing sensor to read out of range — £1,200–£2,200 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Often appears alongside DPF warning light and limp mode
  • Frequently co-occurs with P244A or P2463 — a combination that points to a genuine DPF issue

Do not let anyone sell you a new DPF until the sensor and wiring have been ruled out. Full diagnostic order below.

What P246C Actually Means

In a BMW common-rail diesel, the differential pressure sensor measures the pressure difference across the DPF. The ECU uses this to estimate soot load and trigger regenerations. P246C is set when the sensor signal is either too high, too low, or inconsistent — essentially, the ECU can't trust the reading. This could be because the sensor itself is faulty, the wiring is damaged, or the pressure hoses are blocked or leaking. Importantly, the code does not mean the DPF is blocked — it means the sensor signal is unreliable. That distinction is the difference between a £180 sensor and a £1,500 DPF, and it's why the diagnostic order below is critical. This code is common across N47, B47, N57 and B57 BMW diesel engines, and the diagnostic approach is similar regardless of engine. On N47 and B47 engines, the sensor is relatively accessible; on N57 it can be more buried, but replacement is still routine for a BMW specialist.

Symptoms You'll Notice Alongside It

  • Engine management light (MIL) illuminated, usually amber
  • DPF warning light may also appear, but not always
  • Noticeable power loss or limp mode if the ECU defaults to a safe strategy
  • Reduced fuel economy — the engine may be running slightly rich to protect the DPF
  • In some cases, no drivability issues at all — just the light on the dash

Diagnostic Order — Cheapest First

1

Check the wiring and connector

Inspect for corrosion, damage, or loose pins. Many P246C codes are caused by simple wiring faults that cost almost nothing to fix.

2

Inspect the pressure hoses

Check the hoses connecting the sensor to the DPF for blockages or cracks. Often they get clogged with soot, causing false readings.

3

Live-data the sensor readings

A specialist will compare the pressure reading at idle and under load against expected values. If it's consistently implausible, the sensor is likely faulty.

4

Swap sensor with a known good one (if identical)

If the sensor is identical to another (e.g., on a twin-sensor setup), swapping them can confirm a sensor fault — if the code moves, replace the sensor.

5

Check DPF pressure and soot load

Use diagnostic software to read actual DPF pressure and calculated soot mass. If the pressure is high and soot load is excessive, a forced regen may be needed.

Can You Still Drive It?

Yes — in most cases, it's safe to drive short distances. P246C on its own is not an engine-destroying code. The ECU may limit performance to protect the DPF, but you won't cause immediate damage. However, if the code is accompanied by a flashing glow-plug light or the car enters limp mode, you should avoid motorway driving and get it looked at within a few days. Prolonged driving with a faulty sensor can lead to incorrect regeneration cycles, which may eventually clog the DPF — turning a cheap sensor repair into an expensive filter replacement. On B47 and B57 engines, the sensor is relatively easy to replace; on N57 it's a little more labour-intensive, but still a manageable job for a specialist. Get it diagnosed promptly — the cost gap between a sensor and a DPF is large.

Related Fault Codes

P246C rarely appears alone. Which codes accompany it materially changes the diagnosis.

P244A — DPF Pressure Too High

If this appears with P246C, the sensor is likely reading high pressure; the filter may be genuinely blocked.

P2463 — DPF Soot Accumulation

Confirms that soot load is excessive; the sensor may be reporting accurately.

P2452 — DPF Pressure Sensor Range/Performance

Similar to P246C but may refer to a different sensor or bank; both indicate a sensor circuit issue.

P244B — DPF Pressure Sensor Circuit Fault

Often accompanies P246C when the sensor itself is faulty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with P246C on a BMW?

Yes, for short journeys — but not for extended motorway use if the car has gone into limp mode. Prolonged driving with a faulty sensor can lead to incorrect DPF regenerations and eventual blockages.

Does P246C always mean the DPF needs replacing?

No. In most cases, the sensor or wiring is at fault. Replacement of the sensor (around £180–£380) is far cheaper and resolves the code in the majority of cases.

How much does it cost to fix P246C on a BMW?

It ranges from £100–£250 for wiring/hose repairs, £180–£380 for a sensor replacement, and up to £1,200–£2,200 for a full DPF replacement if the filter is genuinely blocked.

Will clearing the code fix it?

Only temporarily. The code will return if the underlying fault (sensor, wiring, hoses, or DPF) is not addressed. Clearing it also erases freeze-frame data that a specialist would use to pinpoint the cause.
