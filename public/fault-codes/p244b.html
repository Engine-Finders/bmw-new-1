P244B means the engine control unit has detected an out-of-range signal from the DPF differential pressure sensor (Bank 'B'). In most BMW diesels, this is a sensor or wiring fault — not a blocked filter. A replacement sensor typically costs £180–£380 fitted [PATTERN DATA].

  • Most common real cause: a failed differential pressure sensor — £180–£380 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Worst case: a blocked DPF that has damaged the sensor, leading to replacement of both — £1,200–£2,200 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Often appears alongside DPF warning light and limp mode
  • Frequently co-occurs with P2002 or P2463 — a combination that usually points to a genuine DPF blockage

What P244B Actually Means

In a BMW common-rail diesel, two differential pressure sensors (A and B) are sometimes used to monitor soot loading in the DPF. P244B refers specifically to the 'B' sensor — the one that measures pressure across the filter. When the ECU sees a voltage or resistance reading that falls outside the expected range, it sets this code. Importantly, the code does not mean the DPF is blocked — it means the sensor signal is unreliable. The pressure itself might be perfectly normal, but if the sensor is faulty or the wiring is damaged, the ECU can't trust it. That distinction is the difference between a £180 sensor and a £1,500 DPF, and it's why the diagnostic order below is so important. This code is common across N47, B47, N57 and B57 BMW diesel engines, and the diagnostic approach is essentially identical regardless of which engine triggers it. On N47 and B47 engines, the sensor is relatively accessible; on N57 units it can be a little more buried, but replacement is still a routine job for a BMW specialist.

Symptoms You'll Notice Alongside It

  • Engine management light (MIL) illuminated, usually amber
  • DPF warning light may also appear, but not always
  • Noticeable power loss or limp mode if the ECU defaults to a safe strategy
  • Reduced fuel economy — the engine may be running slightly rich to protect the DPF
  • In some cases, no drivability issues at all — just the light on the dash

Diagnostic Order — Cheapest First

1

Check the sensor wiring and connector

Inspect for corrosion, damage, or loose pins. Many P244B codes are caused by simple wiring faults that cost almost nothing to fix.

2

Live-data the sensor readings

A specialist will compare the pressure readings from Sensor A and B at idle and under load. If B is consistently reading implausibly, it's likely faulty.

3

Swap sensor A and B (if identical)

If the sensors are identical, swapping them can confirm a sensor fault — if the code moves to the other bank, replace the sensor.

4

Check DPF pressure and soot load

Use diagnostic software to read actual DPF pressure and calculated soot mass. If the pressure is high and soot load is excessive, a forced regen may be needed.

5

Consider DPF replacement only if all above fail

If the sensor is confirmed good, wiring is intact, and the DPF is truly blocked with ash, replacement is the only option.

Can You Still Drive It?

Yes — in most cases, it's safe to drive short distances. P244B on its own is not an engine-destroying code. The ECU may limit performance to protect the DPF, but you won't cause immediate damage. However, if the code is accompanied by a flashing glow-plug light or the car enters limp mode, you should avoid motorway driving and get it looked at within a few days. Prolonged driving with a faulty sensor can lead to incorrect regeneration cycles, which may eventually clog the DPF — turning a cheap sensor repair into an expensive filter replacement. On B47 and B57 engines, the sensor is relatively easy to replace; on N57 it's a little more labour-intensive, but still a manageable job for a specialist. Get it diagnosed promptly — the cost gap between a sensor and a DPF is large.

Related Fault Codes

P244B rarely appears alone. Which codes accompany it materially changes the diagnosis.

P244A — DPF Pressure Too High

If this appears with P244B, the sensor is likely reading high pressure; the filter may be genuinely blocked.

P2002 — DPF Efficiency Below Threshold

Strongly suggests the DPF is not performing correctly, and the sensor is reporting that accurately.

P2463 — DPF Soot Accumulation

Confirms that soot load is excessive; the sensor may be fine.

P2452 — DPF Pressure Sensor Range/Performance

Similar to P244B but for Sensor A; both indicate a sensor circuit issue.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with P244B on a BMW?

Yes, for short journeys — but not for extended motorway use if the car has gone into limp mode. Prolonged driving with a faulty sensor can lead to incorrect DPF regenerations and eventual blockages.

Does P244B always mean the DPF needs replacing?

No. In most cases, the sensor or wiring is at fault. Replacement of the sensor (around £180–£380) is far cheaper and resolves the code in the majority of cases.

How much does it cost to fix P244B on a BMW?

It ranges from £100–£250 for wiring repairs, £180–£380 for a sensor replacement, and up to £1,200–£2,200 for a full DPF replacement if the filter is genuinely blocked.

Will clearing the code fix it?

Only temporarily. The code will return if the underlying fault (sensor, wiring, or DPF) is not addressed. Clearing it also erases freeze-frame data that a specialist would use to pinpoint the cause.
