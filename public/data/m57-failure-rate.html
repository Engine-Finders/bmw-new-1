M57 Failure Rate in Context

How does the M57 compare to other BMW engines in our 2025 enquiry data?

The M57 is BMW's 3.0-litre straight-six diesel engine, produced from 1998 to 2013. It was replaced by the N57 in 2008 (and gradually phased out over the following years). In our 2025 UK data, the M57 — primarily represented by the N57D30 variant — generated 830 replacement enquiries [EM-VERIFIED], placing it at the third position on our list of BMW engines by request volume. This is lower than the N47 (1,450 enquiries) and B47 (930 enquiries) but higher than the B58 (540 enquiries).

When you consider the M57's enquiry volume against its market share (it was fitted to millions of BMWs across the 3-Series, 5-Series, X3, X5 and 7-Series), it's clear that the N57 has some known issues — particularly timing chain stretch and turbocharger wear. However, the M57 itself is considered one of BMW's most durable diesel engines, with many examples exceeding 200,000 miles [PATTERN DATA].

How Long Do M57 Engines Actually Last?

With proper maintenance, an M57 can exceed 200,000 miles, but the N57's timing chain and turbo issues require attention.

The M57 is considered one of BMW's most durable diesel engines. Many examples exceed 200,000 miles without major issues. The early M57 (pre-2004) is particularly robust, while the later N57 variant has some known weaknesses, particularly timing chain stretch and turbocharger wear. The key to a long N57 life is regular maintenance and monitoring — particularly oil changes at the correct intervals (around 18,000 miles or 2 years) and using the correct oil spec.

Our data suggests that the N57's peak failure window is between 80,000 and 150,000 miles, with timing chain and turbo issues being the most common failure modes. However, many well-maintained examples exceed 200,000 miles without major issues [PATTERN DATA].

Average lifespan: 150,000–200,000 miles with good maintenance [PATTERN DATA].

Our Verdict

7.5 Reliability Score

The M57 is a reliable engine, but the N57 variant has some known weaknesses. The M57 itself is robust and long-lived, but the N57's timing chain and turbo issues can lead to expensive repairs if not addressed early. The main concerns are timing chain stretch, turbocharger wear and oil pump failure, all of which can be catastrophic if ignored.

If you're considering a BMW with an M57 or N57 engine, we would recommend checking the service history and having a pre-purchase inspection. A car with a full service history and no signs of timing chain rattle is a safer buy. This engine is a good buy for most owners, but with caveats.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the M57 engine failure rate?

In our 2025 data, the M57/N57 generated 830 replacement enquiries, making it the third most-enquired BMW engine. This reflects its popularity across a wide range of BMW models as well as its known timing chain and turbo issues. The typical failure mileage is between 80,000 and 150,000 miles.

Is the M57 more reliable than the N57?

Yes, the M57 is generally more reliable than the N57. The early M57 (pre-2004) is particularly robust, while the N57 has more reported cases of timing chain stretch, turbocharger wear and oil pump failure.

What are the common M57 failure points?

The most common M57/N57 failures are timing chain stretch, turbocharger wear and oil pump failure. Other issues include injector failure and swirl flap failure. Timing chain and turbo issues are the most serious and can cause catastrophic engine failure.

How many miles will an M57 engine last?

With proper maintenance, an M57 can last 150,000–200,000 miles. The N57 variant may fail earlier due to timing chain and turbo issues. Regular maintenance and monitoring are key to maximising lifespan.

Should I avoid buying an N57?

Not necessarily, but you should be cautious. An N57 with a full service history and no signs of timing chain rattle is a safer buy. If there's any doubt, have a pre-purchase inspection by a specialist.
