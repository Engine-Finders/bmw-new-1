Insurance Write‑Off Categories – What Each One Means for an Engine Failure

The Association of British Insurers (ABI) defines four categories. For engine‑related claims, the key distinction is between structural damage (Category S) and non‑structural (Category N) – but most engine failures never get classified because they’re wear‑and‑tear, not accident‑related.

A

Category A – Scrap Only

Cat A means the vehicle must be crushed. It’s almost never engine‑related – it requires catastrophic structural damage or fire. If your BMW engine has seized and the car has also been in a major front‑end crash, Cat A is possible, but it’s rare.

B

Category B – Break for Parts

Cat B means the vehicle cannot be repaired but can be dismantled for parts. Engine failures that cause a fire, or that snap the crankshaft and destroy the block and ancillaries, can push a car into Cat B – especially on older, lower‑value models like the E90 3 Series.

S

Category S – Structural

Cat S means the vehicle has sustained structural damage that is repairable. For engine failures, Cat S is rare unless the failure caused a secondary accident – but it can happen if the engine mounts shear and damage the subframe during a breakdown or collision.

N

Category N – Non‑Structural

Cat N is the category most likely to be applied to an engine‑only failure. If the cost of a replacement engine exceeds the vehicle’s pre‑accident value, and there is no structural damage, the car is typically written off as Cat N and can be repaired and returned to the road – but it will carry an insurance marker.

Importantly, most engine failures aren’t insurance claims at all. They are classed as mechanical breakdowns, which are not covered by standard comprehensive policies unless you have specific mechanical breakdown cover. The write‑off categories become relevant when a failure is linked to an accident or when a specialist garage quotes more than the car’s value and the insurer decides to settle.

What to Do Next – If You Think Your BMW Is a Write‑Off

If your insurer has told you the car is a write‑off – or if you’ve been quoted a repair that exceeds 70% of the car’s value – here’s the practical decision tree.

1

Check if you have mechanical breakdown cover

Most standard comprehensive policies do not cover mechanical failure. If you have a separate policy or added cover, check the small print – many exclude “wear and tear” or “pre‑existing conditions.”

2

Get a second quote from a BMW specialist

Main dealer quotes are often 30–50% higher than independent specialists. A used engine from a breaker can be significantly cheaper than a reconditioned unit, and may change the maths. See our Reconditioned vs Used vs Rebuilt guide.

3

Compare the write‑off settlement to the repair cost

If the insurer offers £5,000 and the repair costs £4,500, it’s often better to take the settlement and buy a different car. But if you can buy the car back for a nominal fee (e.g. 20% of the payout) and fit a used engine for £3,000, you may come out ahead – see our Is It Worth Replacing a 3 Series Engine? for a worked example.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Category N write‑off?

Category N (non‑structural) means the vehicle has been written off due to damage that hasn’t affected the structural frame. For engine failures, Cat N is the most common category – it means the repair cost (e.g. a replacement engine) exceeds the insurer’s threshold, but the car can be repaired and returned to the road.

Will my BMW be written off if the timing chain fails?

Not necessarily. If the chain fails and the engine is destroyed, the cost of a replacement can easily exceed the value of an older car – but on a newer, higher‑value BMW (e.g. a G20 3 Series or G01 X3), the repair cost is usually well within the 70% threshold and the car will be repaired. It depends entirely on the value of your specific car.

What is the 70% rule for BMW engine replacement?

The 70% rule is our repair‑to‑value threshold for BMWs: if a repair or replacement engine costs more than 70% of your BMW’s current market value, the honest verdict is usually to scrap or sell for parts rather than repair. It’s a starting guide, not an absolute cut‑off, and we always show the working so you can judge your own case. See our full framework page for the worked example.

Can I buy back a Category N BMW?

Yes – insurers typically allow you to buy back a Cat N vehicle for a percentage of the payout (often 20–30%). You then own the car and can repair it yourself. However, the Cat N marker will stay on the vehicle’s insurance database and future buyers will value it lower – usually by 20–40% compared to a clean example.

Is a reconditioned engine always cheaper than a used one for a write‑off?

No. A used engine is almost always the cheapest supply‑and‑fit option – often 30–40% cheaper than a reconditioned unit. For a Cat N buyback, fitting a used engine is often the most cost‑effective route. But a reconditioned engine comes with a warranty and a known rebuild standard, which might be worth the extra cost if you plan to keep the car long‑term.
