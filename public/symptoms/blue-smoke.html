BMW Blue Smoke From Exhaust

Blue smoke from your BMW's exhaust is a clear sign that engine oil is being burned in the combustion chambers. It's a symptom that can stem from a range of issues, from worn valve stem seals to a failing turbocharger or piston rings. Here's what it means, the most likely causes on BMW engines, and how to narrow it down.

Blue smoke from a BMW exhaust is almost always caused by engine oil entering the combustion chambers. The most common culprits are worn valve stem seals, a failing turbocharger (oil seals leaking), or worn piston rings. It's a serious symptom that, if ignored, can lead to catalytic converter damage, DPF blockage, and eventually engine failure if oil consumption becomes excessive.

  • Most common cause: worn valve stem seals (especially on N63/V8) or turbocharger seal failure (N47/N57) [PATTERN DATA]

What It Looks & Smells Like

Blue smoke is unmistakable. It's a bluish-white haze that lingers in the air, often most noticeable on cold start, after idling for a while, or when you accelerate hard from a standstill. Many owners describe it as a "cloud of blueish smoke" that follows the car, or a "puff of blue" when they pull away from traffic lights. Unlike black smoke (which smells like unburnt fuel) or white smoke (which has a sweet, coolant-like smell), blue smoke has a distinct, acrid smell of burning oil. It's often visible in the rear-view mirror, especially when pulling away from a stop or accelerating onto a motorway. Some owners also report a noticeable drop in oil level between services, as the engine is burning oil at a rate that's higher than normal. If you're topping up oil more frequently than usual, and you see blue smoke, you're almost certainly looking at an oil burning issue.

How to Narrow It Down Yourself (Safe Checks Only)

Before booking in with a specialist, there are a few simple observations you can make that will help you – and your mechanic – narrow down the cause. These checks don't require any tools or lifting the car.

  1. Check when the smoke appears. If it's only on cold start and clears within 30 seconds, it's almost certainly worn valve stem seals. If it appears after the engine is warm, especially when accelerating or decelerating, it could be turbo seals or piston rings.
  2. Monitor oil consumption. If you're adding oil every 500–1,000 miles, that's a strong indicator. Normal BMW oil consumption is up to 1 litre per 1,000 miles (according to BMW), but if it's significantly higher – especially with visible smoke – it's a problem.
  3. Look for oil in the intake pipes. If you can safely access the air intake pipe between the turbo and the intercooler, check for oil residue. A significant amount of oil in this pipe suggests turbocharger seal failure or a faulty PCV system. Only do this when the engine is cold.
  4. Check the PCV system. On many BMW engines, a faulty PCV valve (oil separator) can cause blue smoke. Listen for a whistling noise from the engine, or check for excessive oil in the intake. This is often a cheaper fix than a turbo or valve seals.

Important: Never attempt any checks that require raising the car, removing fuel lines, or working on the high-pressure fuel system. These should always be done by a qualified specialist. If you're not confident, book it in – it's cheaper than a mistake.

When It's Serious vs When It Can Wait

⚡ Serious – Act Now

  • Blue smoke all the time, not just on cold start
  • Smoke accompanied by loss of power or limp mode
  • Oil consumption > 1 litre per 500 miles
  • Check engine light flashing (misfire or knock detected)
  • Smoke from both turbo and valve seals – a common dual failure on N63

Verdict & Next Step

Get it checked as soon as you notice blue smoke that doesn't clear quickly. The most common cause on high-mileage BMWs is worn valve stem seals – a known weak point on the N63 and N47 engines, but also increasingly common on N57, N55 and B58 as they age. It's a labour-intensive but well-understood repair.

If the blue smoke is accompanied by loss of power, knocking, or a flashing check engine light, do not drive the car – call a specialist immediately. Frequently Asked Questions

Is blue smoke from a BMW always a serious problem?

Not always – it could be a simple PCV valve or overfilled oil, which are cheap fixes. However, the most common causes (valve stem seals and turbo seals) are more serious and require professional attention. Never ignore blue smoke.

Can I drive my BMW with blue smoke?

If it's only on cold start and clears quickly, it's safe to drive to a specialist. But if it's constant, especially with power loss or a flashing check engine light, do not drive it – call a specialist for recovery.

How much does it cost to fix blue smoke on a BMW?

It depends on the cause. PCV valve: £200–£600. Turbocharger: £800–£2,500. Valve stem seals on a 4‑cylinder: £1,500–£2,500. On a V8: £3,500–£4,500. The full range is covered in the cost table above.

What causes blue smoke on a BMW?

The most common causes are worn valve stem seals (allowing oil to seep into the combustion chambers), a failing turbocharger oil seal, worn piston rings, or a faulty PCV valve. Less common causes include overfilled oil and valve guide wear.

Why does my BMW smoke blue on cold start?

This is the classic symptom of worn valve stem seals. When the engine is off, oil seeps past the seals and pools in the cylinders. On cold start, this oil burns off, producing a puff of blue smoke that clears after a few seconds.

Does blue smoke fail an MOT?

Yes – excessive blue smoke is an automatic MOT failure for both diesel and petrol vehicles. If your BMW is producing visible blue smoke, it will fail the emissions test.

Can a DPF cause blue smoke?

No – DPF issues typically cause black smoke (blockage) or white smoke (regeneration). Blue smoke is always oil-related, not DPF-related.

How do I know if it's valve seals or piston rings?

Valve seals usually cause blue smoke on cold start that clears. Piston rings cause blue smoke all the time, especially under acceleration. A compression test and leak-down test can confirm which it is.
