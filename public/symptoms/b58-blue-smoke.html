What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general blue smoke guide covers several possible causes across the whole BMW range, on the B58 the picture narrows considerably. This turbocharged petrol engine's dominant failure signature is a bluish-white cloud of smoke from the exhaust, often most noticeable on start-up, after idling, or under hard acceleration. It is distinct from the black smoke of over-fuelling and the white smoke of coolant ingress. On the B58, blue smoke almost always points to engine oil being drawn into the combustion chamber, and the most common culprits are a failed PCV valve (which allows crankcase oil to be sucked into the intake), worn valve stem seals (which leak oil past the valves), or a turbocharger oil seal leak — all of which are known issues on higher-mileage units. This symptom is often the first sign of a failing PCV on the B58, and catching it early can save you from catalytic converter damage.

Why This Engine In Particular

The B58's turbocharged design and high-performance nature make it more sensitive to oil control issues than naturally aspirated engines. The PCV system is integrated into the valve cover and is known to fail on higher-mileage B58 units, especially on cars that have seen frequent short trips or track use. When the PCV diaphragm tears or the valve sticks, crankcase pressure increases, forcing oil mist into the intake manifold — which then burns in the cylinders and exits as blue smoke. Similarly, the valve stem seals on the B58 are made of a material that can harden over time, especially with extended oil change intervals, leading to oil leakage past the valve guides. The turbocharger's oil seals can also wear, particularly if the engine has been run hard and hot. This is a design characteristic of this particular engine family, and the same symptom on, say, an earlier N55 would point to a similar set of causes, but the B58's integrated PCV makes replacement more labour-intensive.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, rule out cheaper alternatives. Check the oil level — overfilling can cause blue smoke. Also check the PCV system: a failed PCV often makes a whistling noise from the valve cover or causes a rough idle. If the blue smoke is worse on cold start and clears as the engine warms, it's likely valve stem seals. If it's worse under boost, suspect turbo seals. A leak-down test can help differentiate between piston rings and valve seals. If the blue smoke persists, especially if it's accompanied by a loss of power, hesitation, or a check engine light, it's time for a professional diagnosis. A specialist will perform a compression test, leak-down test, and inspect the PCV system and turbocharger. Do not ignore blue smoke; it will only get worse and can lead to catalytic converter damage and reduced engine performance.

Verdict

Get it diagnosed by a specialist as soon as you notice blue smoke. The B58's blue smoke is a clear sign of oil burning, and early intervention can save you from a much larger bill. If it's a simple PCV valve, the repair is moderate and straightforward. If it's valve stem seals or turbo seals, catching it early can prevent secondary damage to the catalytic converter. Frequently Asked Questions

Is blue smoke from a B58 always a valve stem seal issue?

Not always. While valve stem seals are a common cause, a failed PCV valve or turbocharger oil seals can also produce blue smoke. A specialist can differentiate with a leak-down test and visual inspection.

At what mileage does a B58 typically start producing blue smoke?

Most cases in our data occur between 60,000 and 120,000 miles, though this is heavily dependent on service history and driving conditions. Regular oil changes and using the correct oil spec can significantly reduce the risk.

Can I drive a B58 with blue smoke?

It's not recommended. Continued driving with blue smoke can quickly damage the catalytic converter and oxygen sensors, leading to much more expensive repairs. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

How much does it cost to fix blue smoke on a B58?

A PCV valve replacement typically costs £400–£800 at an independent specialist. Valve stem seals can cost £1,500–£3,000. If the turbocharger is at fault, expect £1,500–£3,000. If the engine has suffered severe damage, you're looking at £8,000–£12,000 for a reconditioned engine supply and fit.
