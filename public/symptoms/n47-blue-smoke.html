What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general blue smoke guide covers several possible causes across the whole BMW range, on the N47 the picture narrows considerably. This diesel engine's dominant failure signature is a thick, bluish cloud of smoke from the exhaust, often most noticeable under acceleration or after the engine has been idling, accompanied by a distinct burning oil smell. It is distinct from the black smoke of over-fuelling (injectors, MAF) and the white smoke of coolant ingress (head gasket). On the N47, blue smoke almost always points to oil entering the combustion chamber, and the most common culprits are a failing turbocharger seal, worn piston rings, or valve stem seals — all of which are known issues on higher-mileage units.

Why This Engine In Particular

The N47's turbocharger is oil-cooled and relies on a seal to keep oil out of the intake and exhaust tracts. Over time, this seal can fail, allowing oil to be drawn into the turbocharger and burned in the engine, producing the characteristic blue smoke. Additionally, the N47's piston rings can wear, allowing oil to bypass them and enter the combustion chamber. Valve stem seals can also harden and fail, especially on higher-mileage engines, allowing oil to seep past the valve guides. This is a design characteristic of this particular engine family, and the same symptom on, say, a B58 petrol would point to a very different set of causes.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, rule out cheaper alternatives. Check the PCV (positive crankcase ventilation) system — a blocked PCV can cause oil to be drawn into the intake, mimicking a turbo seal failure. Replacing the PCV valve is a relatively low-cost fix. If the blue smoke persists, especially if it's accompanied by a loss of power, hesitation, or a check engine light, it's time for a professional diagnosis. A specialist will perform a turbocharger inspection, a cylinder compression test, and a leak-down test to pinpoint the source. Do not ignore blue smoke; it will only get worse and can lead to a blocked DPF and catalytic converter.

Verdict

Get it diagnosed by a specialist as soon as you notice blue smoke. The N47's blue smoke is a clear sign of oil burning, and early intervention can save you from a much larger bill. If it's a simple PCV valve, the repair is cheap and straightforward. If it's a turbocharger or piston ring issue, catching it early can prevent secondary damage to the DPF and catalytic converter. Frequently Asked Questions

Is blue smoke from an N47 always a turbocharger issue?

It's the most common cause, but not the only one. A failing PCV valve, worn piston rings, or valve stem seals can also cause blue smoke. A specialist can diagnose the exact cause with a compression test and turbocharger inspection.

At what mileage does an N47 typically start producing blue smoke?

Most cases in our data occur between 80,000 and 150,000 miles, though this is heavily dependent on service history and driving conditions. Regular oil changes with the correct BMW Longlife-04 spec oil can significantly reduce the risk.

Can I drive an N47 with blue smoke?

It's not recommended. Continued driving with blue smoke can quickly block the DPF and damage the catalytic converter, leading to much more expensive repairs. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

How much does it cost to fix blue smoke on an N47?

A PCV valve replacement costs around £150–£300. A turbocharger rebuild or replacement typically costs £1,200–£2,500. If the engine needs a rebuild or replacement, you're looking at £3,500–£6,500.

What is the 70% scrap threshold for BMW?

BMW's repair-or-scrap threshold is 70% of the vehicle's market value. If the fitted replacement cost exceeds 70% of what your BMW is worth, it may be financially wiser to scrap or sell the car rather than repair. Use the Repair vs. Replace Calculator to decide.
