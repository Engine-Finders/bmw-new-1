BMW N55 Blue Smoke From Exhaust

Blue smoke on the N55 is a different conversation to the same symptom on any other BMW engine. Here's exactly what it means on this twin-turbo straight-six, the components most likely to be responsible, and real cost data if it turns out to be the fault this engine is known for.

On the BMW N55 specifically, blue smoke from the exhaust is a clear sign of oil burning — most commonly caused by a faulty turbocharger seal, worn valve stem seals, or a failing PCV system. It's a serious issue that, if ignored, can lead to carbon buildup, catalytic converter damage, and eventual engine failure.

What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general blue smoke guide covers several possible causes across the whole BMW range, on the N55 the picture narrows considerably. This twin-turbo straight-six's dominant failure signature is a thick, blue-tinged cloud of smoke from the exhaust, often most noticeable on startup or after idling, accompanied by a drop in oil level and a distinct burning oil smell. It is distinct from the black smoke of over-fuelling (injector issues) and the white smoke of coolant ingress (head gasket). On the N55, blue smoke almost always points to oil entering the combustion chamber, and the most common culprits are faulty turbocharger seals, worn valve stem seals, or a blocked PCV system — all of which are known issues on higher-mileage units.

Why This Engine In Particular

The N55's twin-turbo design and direct injection system are precisely what make it so powerful when it's working, and precisely what cause such dramatic blue smoke when it isn't. The turbochargers operate at extremely high temperatures, and the seals can fail over time, allowing oil to pass into the exhaust. The valve stem seals can also wear, letting oil seep past the valves and into the cylinders. Additionally, the PCV system, which regulates crankcase pressure, can become blocked, forcing oil past the turbo seals. This is a design characteristic of this particular engine, and the same symptom on, say, a B58 would point to a very different set of causes.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, rule out cheaper alternatives. Check the oil level — overfilling can cause blue smoke. Also check for any leaks in the turbo oil lines. If the blue smoke is persistent, especially if it's accompanied by a loss of power, hesitation, or a check engine light, it's time for a professional diagnosis. A specialist will perform a compression test, a leak-down test, and a boost pressure test to check the turbochargers. Do not ignore blue smoke; it will only get worse and can lead to carbon buildup on the valves and catalytic converter damage.

Verdict

Get it diagnosed by a specialist as soon as you notice blue smoke. The N55's blue smoke is a clear sign of oil burning, and early intervention can save you from a much larger bill. If it's a simple PCV or oil leak, the repair is cheap and straightforward. If it's a turbocharger or valve stem seal issue, catching it early can prevent secondary damage to the catalytic converters. Frequently Asked Questions

Is blue smoke from an N55 always a turbo seal issue?

It's the most common cause, but not the only one. Worn valve stem seals, a blocked PCV system, or even a minor oil leak can also cause blue smoke. A specialist can diagnose the exact cause with a compression test and leak-down test.

At what mileage does an N55 typically start producing blue smoke?

Most cases in our data occur between 70,000 and 120,000 miles, though this is heavily dependent on service history and driving conditions. Regular oil changes and using good quality oil can significantly reduce the risk.

Can I drive an N55 with blue smoke?

It's not recommended. Continued driving with blue smoke can quickly block the catalytic converters and damage the oxygen sensors, leading to much more expensive repairs. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

How much does it cost to fix blue smoke on an N55?

A turbocharger replacement typically costs £1,200–£2,500 at an independent specialist. Valve stem seal replacement can cost £1,500–£3,000. If the engine has suffered severe damage, you're looking at £3,500–£6,000 for a reconditioned engine supply and fit.
